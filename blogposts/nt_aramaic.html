Aramaic in the New Testament

August 6, 2026

The Gospels were written down in ancient Greek. Like it or not, this was the lingua franca of the Eastern Mediterranean for about a millenium. Proto-Christians wanted to communicate their message to the masses, and Koinē Greek (a form of ancient Greek adapted and simplified from earlier dialects that was heavily in use in the Classical period) was the way to do it.

But of course, the characters in the Gospels were Judaean men, including Galileans such as Jesus himself. If they knew any Greek, it was not their mother tongue, and it is unlikely they used it in casual conversation amongst themselves.

Interestingly, the Gospels themselves give us direct evidence that this was the case. Several times the Gospels famously provide phrases transliterated (not translated) from Aramaic, which the text calls Ἑβραϊστι (Hebraïsti). This also indicates that Aramaic had acquired a particularly Jewish character by this late time.

Transliterations

A number of transliterations stand out. The most common ones are the consistent use of ῥαββεί (rhabbéi, rabbi) by the apostles to address their teacher, and Jesus's use of ἀμήν (amen).

For the following one, the context is that Jesus has been led into the house of a man named Jaïrus (evidently יאיר) whose daughter has died.

καὶ κρατήσας τῆς χειρὸς τοῦ παιδίου λέγει αὐτῇ, Ταλιθα κουμ, ὅ ἐστιν μεθερμηνευόμενον Τὸ κοράσιον, σοὶ λέγω, ἔγειρε.
Mark 5:41
And taking the child's hand, he said to her, Talitha kum, which, translated, is "Girl, I tell you, wake up!"
Mark 5:41 (translated supplied by me)

Some manuscripts have kumi. In any case, it's clearly a Semitic phrase: טליתא קום.

Another instance we find is also in Mark. Jesus is trying to heal a deaf man:

καὶ ἀναβλέψας εἰς τὸν οὐρανὸν ἐστέναξεν, καὶ λέγει αὐτῷ, Εφφαθα, ὅ ἐστιν, Διανοίχθητι.
Mark 7:33
And looking up to the heavens, he sighed, and said to him, Ephphatha, which is, "Be opened".
Mark 7:33 (translated supplied by me)

This comes from the Semitic root פתח meaning "open." It seems that the tau that would normally precede the pe (Aramaic אתפתח) has assimilated into it (hence the geminated phi).

The final (and arguably most famous) use of Aramaic I'll list is this:

καὶ τῇ ἐνάτῃ ὥρᾳ ἐβόησεν ὁ Ἰησοῦς φωνῇ μεγάλῃ, Ελωι ελωι λεμα σαβαχθανι; ὅ ἐστιν μεθερμηνευόμενον Ὁ θεός μου ὁ θεός μου, εἰς τί ἐγκατέλιπές με;
Mark 15:34
And at the ninth hour, Jesus cried out in a great voice, "Eloï, eloï, lema sabachthani?" which is, translated, "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?"
Mark 15:34 (translation supplied by me)

The exclamation also appears in Matthew 27:46, with "my God" rendered as ηλι (ēli).

It is lifted from Psalm 22:1 but translated from the original Hebrew into Aramaic:

אֵלִי אֵלִי לָמָה עֲזַבְתָּנִי
Psalm 22:1
Eli eli lama 'azavtani
Psalm 22:1 (transliterated)

Polysemy

The other side of this coin is the how knowledge of Aramaic can give us context about puns and wordplay. A cool one is that both Matthew and Luke give a version of the Lord's prayer, but they differ as follows (among other ways):

καὶ ἄφες ἡμῖν τὰ ὀφειλήματα ἡμῶν, ὡς καὶ ἡμεῖς ἀφήκαμεν τοῖς ὀφειλέταις ἡμῶν
Matthew 6:12
And forgive us our debts, as we too have forgiven our debtors
Matthew 6:12 (translation supplied by me)

Whereas Luke:

καὶ ἄφες ἡμῖν τὰς ἁμαρτίας ἡμῶν, καὶ γὰρ αὐτοὶ ἀφίομεν παντὶ ὀφείλοντι ἡμῖν· καὶ μὴ εἰσενέγκῃς ἡμᾶς εἰς πειρασμόν.
Luke 11:4
And forgive us our sins, as we too forgive all those who owe us: and lead us not into temptation.
Luke 11:4 (translation supplied by me)

The confusion between "debt" and "sin" is likely explained by the Aramaic word חובא (ħobba). Literally, it means an obligation or debt, but it also has a sense of sin. This could underly the choices of Matthew and Luke to translate the words of Jesus differently.
